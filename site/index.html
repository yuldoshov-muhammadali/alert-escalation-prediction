1Overview and approach

Each alert comes with ~180 days of the customer's transaction history. The task is to rank alerts by the probability that an analyst escalates them (eskalatsiya = 1); submissions are scored by ROC-AUC.

Key takeaways

The escalation signal is weak and sits in the level of amounts by transaction type and direction: high bank-transfer amounts go with fewer escalations, while high card outflows and high cash inflows go with more. The dramatic pre-alert burst, the transaction sequence and the long-term history carry almost no additional information. The dependence is close to linear and additive, so a logistic regression on 65 aggregates is the strongest single model (0.6453). A pre-registered rank-average of LR, LightGBM and CatBoost (50/25/25) reaches 0.6478.

Pipeline

  1. Anchor rule — measure every signal's history back from max(signal date 00:00, end of the last transaction's day); nothing after the anchor exists.
  2. 65 baseline features — one function applied identically to train and test (no target use).
  3. Validation — repeated stratified K-fold (5 folds × 3 repeats, seed 42), paired fold comparisons, strict acceptance rule, feature selection only inside folds.
  4. Models — logistic regression, LightGBM, CatBoost on the same folds.
  5. Ensemble — in-fold rank-average 50/25/25, then quantile-mapped onto the LR prediction distribution.

4Insights from transaction histories

What moves the escalation rate, from the EDA and from the logistic-regression coefficients (standardised; every sign identical in 15 of 15 fold models).

More escalation

  • Higher average cash inflow amount (coef +0.224).
  • Wider spread and higher top-end of card amounts (card std +0.134, card max +0.124, card 90th percentile +0.106).
  • Higher average card outflow amount (+0.120).
  • Slightly more transactions: escalation rises from 15.5% in the lowest to 19.0% in the highest volume quintile.

Less escalation

  • Higher average bank-transfer outflow (−0.217) and inflow (−0.142) amounts.
  • Higher bank-transfer 90th percentile (−0.147) and spread (−0.143).
  • “Floor” bank inflows (−0.140): 5 117 transactions lie within 0.01 of the global minimum amount (−2.912), and 100% of them are bank-transfer inflows. Signals with such a transaction (12.6%) escalate at 10.0% vs 18.2% for the rest.

7Validation and modelling

Validation protocol

  • Repeated stratified K-fold: 5 folds × 3 repeats, seed 42 — the same 15 folds for every model.
  • Metric: mean ± std of the 15 fold AUCs; comparisons are paired by fold and reported per repeat.
  • Strict acceptance rule: a change must gain ≥ 0.003 AUC, win in ≥ 12 of 15 folds and have the same sign in all 3 repeats.
  • Any feature selection happens inside the training folds.

A leak we caught ourselves

L1-regularised selection of 65 features on the full training set, followed by cross-validation, gave 0.6548 and “won” in 15/15 folds. Repeating the selection inside each fold gives 0.6441. The +0.0107 was pure selection optimism — the list was discarded and the in-fold rule became mandatory.

How the ensemble is built

  • Within each validation fold, each model's predictions are converted to ranks and averaged 0.50 · LR + 0.25 · LGB + 0.25 · CAT. Weights were fixed before looking at the blend, so there is no weight-tuning optimism.
  • Gain over LR: +0.0025 ± 0.0054, better in 10 of 15 folds, positive in all three repeats (+0.0025 / +0.0014 / +0.0034). It does not pass the strict rule but is consistently positive.
  • It helps because the models disagree: test-prediction Spearman correlation LR–LightGBM 0.897, LR–CatBoost 0.894, LightGBM–CatBoost 0.980.
  • For the test set, the blended ranking is quantile-mapped onto the LR test predictions: order comes from the ensemble, values from the calibrated LR (all probabilities stay in 0.0011–0.5983).

9Limitations and conclusion

Noise level. With 6 000 test signals, the standard error of a test AUC around 0.65 is ≈ 0.010 (Hanley–McNeil approximation at a prevalence similar to train). The ensemble's +0.0025 over LR is well inside that noise; differences between our single models are too.

What did not work

  • Burst composition, transaction sequences and long-term trends.
  • Amount histograms, splines and pairwise interactions.
  • More features in general: none of the 14 feature-group tests passed the acceptance rule.

What we would do next

  • A sequence model on the last 512 transactions (the data-preparation script exists and was tested on a sample; no model was trained).
  • Choose ensemble weights with nested cross-validation instead of fixing them.
  • Understand the generator behind the “floor” and cap amounts — the clearest structure we found.

Conclusion: a small, well-validated linear model on type-level amount aggregates captures almost all the signal we could find; the final ensemble adds a small but consistent gain, and every number here is backed by paired, repeated cross-validation.
